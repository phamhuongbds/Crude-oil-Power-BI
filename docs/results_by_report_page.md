# Results by report page

What each page of the Power BI report shows. All figures come from the report and cover trading days up to 28 October 2026. The analysis is descriptive: it shows what happened and how markets moved together, not what caused it.

## Page 1: Overview

**What the page shows.** WTI closed at \$102.78 on 28 October 2026. That is 79.0% above the 2025 close of \$57.42 and 16.9% below the highest close in the dataset, \$123.70 on 8 March 2022. Thirty-day annualised volatility is 39.2%: above the 32% to 35% of calm periods, far below the crisis peaks of about 220% in April 2020 and about 110% in 2026. The event dots cluster in 2020, 2022 and 2026, the three years of large shocks. The one point below zero is 20 April 2020, when the front-month contract closed at -\$37.63.

**Implication.** The current price is not the main finding. What matters is how far it is from the price the budget assumed. The market price is 79% above the end-2025 price of \$57.42, so a budget built on that price is far too low. A 30-day volatility of 39%, above the 32% to 35% of calm periods, shows that the market is still unsettled. The budget should be re-based on the current price range, and the high-volatility case should serve as the planning case until volatility returns to calm levels.

**What to keep in mind.** A year-to-date figure depends on its start date. 2025 ended close to its low (the lowest close of 2025 was \$55.27 on 16 December), so +79% flatters the move; against the end-2024 close of \$71.72 the rise is +43%. The drawdown card also takes the highest close of the whole ten years as its peak, so it changes if the window changes.

## Page 2: Risk (questions 1 and 2)

**Volatility (question 1).** Three periods are calm, at 31.7% to 35.1%. Two are shocks: the Ukraine war spike at 51.0% and the Hormuz crisis at 65.6%. The COVID shock is in a class of its own at 99.8%, with the 30-day figure peaking near 220%. The Hormuz crisis is the second most volatile period despite being the shortest (168 trading days). Within it WTI rose from \$67.02 on 27 February to \$112.95 on 7 April (+68.5%), fell to \$68.55 on 6 July (-39.3%) and climbed back to \$102.78 on 28 October (+49.9%). As a rough rule, annualised volatility divided by 3.5 (the square root of 12) gives a typical monthly move: about 9% to 10% in the calm periods and about 19% in the Hormuz crisis. This assumes returns are roughly bell-shaped, which oil returns are not, so treat it as an order of magnitude.

**Falls and recovery (question 2).** The -100% in the COVID period is an artefact of the negative price on 20 April 2020: the measure is capped at -100%, while the real fall was from about \$63 in January 2020 to below zero. The other deep falls were:

- Pre-COVID: -44.3%, from \$76.41 on 3 October 2018 to \$42.53 on 24 December 2018.

- Ukraine war spike: -42.6%, from \$123.70 on 8 March 2022 to \$71.02 on 9 December 2022.

- Post-spike normalisation: -41.0%, from \$93.68 on 27 September 2023 to \$55.27 on 16 December 2025. At that low the price was 55% below the March 2022 high.

Recovery took very different lengths. The pre-COVID high of \$63.27 (6 January 2020) was regained on 25 February 2021, after 13 months. The 2018 peak of \$76.41 was regained on 4 October 2021, after three years. The March 2022 high has not been regained in 4.6 years: the highest close since is \$122.11 on 8 June 2022, and the 2026 high is \$112.95.

**Implication.** Volatility is not one number. A typical monthly move was about 10% in calm periods and nearly double in the Hormuz crisis, so a budget needs a normal range and a stress range. Falls can last for years (the 2022 peak is still not regained), so a peak price is a poor anchor, and the -100% of the COVID period should not be used as a planning figure.

**What to keep in mind.** Volatility counts rises and falls alike, but a fuel buyer is hurt only by rises. Upward and downward volatility were not separated, although that split would be the better measure of budget risk. The six periods were set around known events, so the contrast between them is partly a matter of choice. The Hormuz result rests on 168 trading days, and a drawdown shows how far the price fell, not how long it stayed low.

## Page 3: Euro view (question 3)

**What the page shows.** In every year the euro price was below the dollar price, by 5% to 15%, and by 10.5% over the whole period. The gap is the exchange rate and nothing else: each year's gap equals 1 divided by the average EUR/USD, minus 1, to within half a point (2022: -4.95% in the report, -5.0% by this formula; the small differences arise because the report averages daily ratios). The two price lines in the monthly chart move together and the gap between them widens and narrows with EUR/USD.

**Two years show how the exchange rate can help or hurt.**

- 2022: the euro weakened to an average of 1.0531 (it fell below parity in the autumn) just as oil spiked. The average dollar price rose 38.5% over 2021 (\$68.11 to \$94.33), but the average euro price rose 55.4% (€57.70 to €89.66). The weak euro added about 17 points to the increase.

- 2026 so far: the dollar price is 31.3% above 2025 (\$64.74 to \$84.98), the euro price 27.8% above (€57.53 to €73.50). A stronger euro (1.1575 against 1.1306) absorbed about 3.5 points.

**Implication.** Budget in euros and treat EUR/USD as a second risk next to the oil price. The euro cushion was 5% to 15% in every year, but it is not constant: in 2022 the euro weakened just as oil spiked, and the buyer was hit by both. A rise in the dollar price tells a euro buyer little without the exchange rate.

**What to keep in mind.** Yearly averages hide timing: a company that buys monthly pays the monthly average, and one that hedges the exchange rate does not feel the swing at all. The reason for the weak euro in 2022 was not tested. The energy shock may have weakened the euro, or both may reflect a common cause, so the report shows the two moving together, not one causing the other. The 2026 figures cover ten months only.

## Page 4: Macro drivers (question 4)

The page shows the correlation of daily WTI log returns with each series, by period.

**What the page shows.**

- Brent and WTI are close to one market (0.85 to 0.97 in every period), so the WTI price is a good stand-in for the price a European buyer faces.

- Energy shares (XLE) follow oil clearly but only partly (0.46 to 0.67), and the link is stable.

- The German DAX is the series that changes. In the first three periods it moved slightly with oil (0.24 to 0.27), in the next two it was unrelated, and during the Hormuz crisis it moved against oil (-0.59): when oil rose because supply was cut, German shares fell.

- The dollar index and EUR/USD have weak and unstable links. The signs flip between periods, and the largest values (DXY 0.31, EUR/USD -0.20) both come from the Hormuz crisis.

- Rates and inflation (line charts): the Fed funds rate stayed near zero from 2020 to early 2022 and rose to about 5.3% in 2023 and 2024; the ECB deposit rate was negative until mid-2022 and peaked near 4.0% in 2024. US inflation peaked at about 9% in mid-2022 and euro-area inflation at about 10.6% in autumn 2022, a few months after oil peaked in March 2022. In 2026 inflation is rising again: US headline CPI went from 2.4% in January to 3.4% in August, and euro-area inflation was 3.8% in September.

**Implication.** Do not rely on a single relationship. Brent can stand in for WTI in any period (0.85 to 0.97), but the DAX and dollar links changed sign between periods, so a hedge or forecast built on calm-period correlations would have failed in 2026. Relationships have to be tested in the stress period, not on the average.

**What to keep in mind.** Same-day correlations of daily returns were measured, so effects that take months, such as oil feeding into inflation, are invisible to the matrix and are only suggested by the line charts. Seven rows and five series make 35 coefficients, and with only 168 days in the Hormuz period a value such as DXY 0.31 may be chance. The period boundaries were chosen by judgement, and other dates could give other numbers.

**Limits.** The Hormuz period has 168 trading days, so its correlations are less certain than those of the longer periods. Markets close at different times (the DAX before US oil settles), which lowers same-day correlations. Correlation is not causation.

## Page 5: Events (question 5)

**What the page shows.** The bar chart covers the 28 of the 40 events that have an exact day and are not market milestones. It shows the price change from the close before the event to the fourth trading day after it. Seventeen events were followed by a rise and eleven by a fall, ranging from +35.6% to -23.1%.

- Largest rises: military action in the Middle East and closure of the Strait of Hormuz (+35.6%), Russia's invasion of Ukraine (+20.1%), the Fed's first rate hike (+15.9%), OPEC's first output cut since 2008 (+12.6%) and Israel's strike on Iran in June 2025 (+10.1%).

- Largest falls: the collapse of the OPEC+ talks and the Saudi price war (-23.1%), the WHO pandemic declaration (-21.6%), the record OPEC+ cut of April 2020 (-19.7%), the US-led reserve release of November 2021 (-13.8%) and the US ban on Russian oil imports (-13.7%).

The drill-through page shows the path day by day, with the day before the event set to 100. After Hormuz the index was 135.6 on day 4 and 153.5 on day 20, so the rise did not fade. After the WHO declaration it was 78.4 on day 4 and 73.0 on day 20.

**Patterns.** Three patterns stand out.

- A real physical disruption lifted prices and kept them up (Hormuz, Ukraine, the Iran strikes).

- Shocks to demand and price wars pushed prices down by about 20% in a week (COVID, the OPEC+ collapse).

- The headline direction did not decide the outcome. The record OPEC+ output cut of April 2020 sounds bullish, yet the price fell 19.7% because the loss of demand was larger. The US ban on Russian oil fell on the day of the 2022 high, and the price fell 13.7% in the window that followed.

**Caution.** An event window return is not the effect of the event. The Fed's first hike on 16 March 2022 shows +15.9%, but the window starts at \$96.44, already 22% below the 8 March high of \$123.70, and the price was rebounding from the war shock. The rise reflects that rebound, not the rate decision. Windows also overlap in crisis weeks, and the drill-through page lists the source and verification status of each event.

**Implication.** Classify an event by its mechanism, not its headline. A real loss of supply lifted prices and kept them up, a demand shock or price war cut them by about 20% in a week, and announcements such as output cuts are ambiguous. A risk log for fuel buying should first ask whether an event removes barrels or removes demand, and only then rank the news by size.

**What to keep in mind.** The 40 events were chosen after the price history was known, so the list leans towards events that moved the market. A fair test would use a list fixed in advance, for example every OPEC+ meeting. Windows overlap and carry other news, as the Fed example shows. For that reason individual bars are illustrations, not measurements.

## Page 6: Budget (question 6)

**What the page shows.** The page applies the oil price change to the part of the budget that follows crude: extra cost = annual budget × crude-linked share × price change. With the defaults (€1,000,000 budget, 40% crude-linked share) the result is a straight line: every 10 points of oil price change is worth €40,000, so a 30% fall saves €120,000, a 20% rise costs €80,000 and a 50% rise costs €200,000.

**Real moves from the earlier pages, applied to the same budget (40% share).**

| Move | Size | Extra cost |
|---|---|---|
| A typical month in a calm period (page 2) | about 10% | about €40,000 |
| A typical month in the Hormuz crisis (page 2) | about 19% | about €76,000 |
| The 2026 euro average against 2025 (page 3) | +27.8% | about €111,000 |
| First five trading days after the Hormuz closure (page 5) | +35.6% | about €142,500 |
| The 2022 euro average against 2021 (page 3) | +55.4% | about €221,600 |

**Implication.** The crude-linked share, not a price forecast, is the lever the company controls. A typical month's swing costs about 4% of the budget in calm times and about 8% in a crisis (at a 40% share), so a contingency of that order covers a normal move. Lowering the share, for example with fixed-price clauses, cuts every outcome in proportion. The page is a tool for scale, not a finding, because the line is straight by design.

**What to keep in mind.** The 40% share is an assumption, not a measurement, so the euro figures are illustrative. The model passes oil changes straight to the fuel price, but real fuel prices lag, are cushioned by taxes and may be hedged. A typical swing covers a normal month, not a bad one. German pump price data would replace the assumed share with a measured pass-through.

**Limits.** The model assumes the fuel price moves one for one with crude on the linked share and ignores taxes, margins, hedging and delivery lags. Apply the shock to the euro price, since the budget is in euros.

## Practical conclusions

For a German company with fuel costs, the six pages lead to three practical conclusions.

1.  **Plan with ranges, not one number.** Use a different range for calm and crisis conditions: a typical monthly move of about 10% in calm periods and about 19% in a crisis, and falls of 40% or more from a peak that can last years.

2.  **Budget in euros and watch EUR/USD.** The exchange rate cut the euro price by 5% to 15% in every year and, in 2022, made the euro rise 17 points larger than the dollar rise.

3.  **Decide how much of the cost follows crude.** The budget page shows that this share scales every outcome. Reducing it, for example with fixed-price clauses, is as effective as predicting the price.

## Limits of the analysis

- The data are futures prices (WTI front month), not pump prices. The link between the two, including taxes and margins, is not measured.

- The report is descriptive. Correlations and event windows show what moved together, not what caused what, and the event windows overlap in crisis weeks.

- Some inputs are weaker than others: the Hormuz period has 168 trading days and 2026 is a partial year. Event dates, categories and importance are judgement, and 3 of the 40 events come from the price data itself rather than an outside source.

- The COVID period contains a negative price (20 April 2020), so its returns are blank on that day and its drawdown is capped at -100%.

- Monthly inflation is dated to the first of the month, and markets close at different times, which lowers same-day correlations.

- The budget model is a straight line by design and ignores hedging, taxes and delivery lags.

- Nothing here is a price forecast or investment advice.
